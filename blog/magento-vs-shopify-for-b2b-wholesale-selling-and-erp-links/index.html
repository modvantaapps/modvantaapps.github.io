Magento vs Shopify for B2B: Wholesale Selling and ERP Links

Magento vs Shopify for B2B: how each handles wholesale pricing, company accounts and payment terms, and what it takes to connect either one to an ERP.

Picture a distributor that sells industrial supplies to a few hundred trade customers. Orders come in by email, phone and a spreadsheet that one sales rep updates on Fridays. Each customer has its own price list, a few have 60-day terms, and the warehouse team retypes every order into the ERP. The owner wants a proper wholesale web shop, and the first question on the table is the one that brought you here: Magento or Shopify?

We get asked this a lot. We build for both platforms and we connect both to Odoo, so we don't have a horse in the race. What we can tell you is where each one fits, where each one hurts, and why the ERP side of the decision matters more than most people expect.

What B2B buyers actually need from a store

Retail shoppers want a nice product page and a fast checkout. Trade buyers want something different. In our experience, a wholesale store has to handle most of these:

  • Customer-specific pricing. Contract prices, tiered discounts by volume, or a whole price list per customer group.
  • Company accounts. One business, several buyers, maybe an approver who signs off on orders above a certain amount.
  • Payment on terms. Invoice now, pay in 30 or 60 days, with a credit limit somewhere in the background.
  • Quick reordering. Upload a CSV, reorder last month's order, or type SKUs into a grid.
  • Minimums and pack sizes. You sell in cases of 12, not single units.
  • Tax handling for businesses. Exemptions, reverse charge or VAT number checks, depending on where you and your buyers are. These rules differ by country and change over time, so confirm the details with a local accountant or your tax authority before you set anything up.

Both platforms can do most of this. They just get there in very different ways.

Magento for B2B: deep, flexible, and more work

Magento comes in two forms. Magento Open Source is free to install, and Adobe Commerce is the paid, licensed version. The difference matters a lot for B2B, because the native B2B module (company accounts, shared catalogs, quote requests, requisition lists, purchase approvals) ships with Adobe Commerce. On Open Source you'd get those through third-party extensions or custom development.

Where Magento shines is control. You own the code and the database, so if your pricing logic is strange, and wholesale pricing usually is, you can build exactly what you need. We've seen rules like "this customer gets list price minus 18%, except on brand X, where they get the contract price, unless the order is over a pallet." That's annoying on any platform. On Magento, it's a module. You write it, test it, and it does what you said.

The cost of that freedom is ownership. You need hosting, a developer who knows Magento properly, and a plan for upgrades and security patches. Extensions from different vendors don't always play well together, and a poorly written one can slow the whole store down. When we build Magento work, we write it to Adobe's coding standards for exactly that reason. You can see what that covers on our Magento development page.

Magento makes sense when:

  1. Your catalogue is big or complicated (configurable products, lots of attributes, several store views or languages).
  2. Your pricing and approval rules don't fit any standard template.
  3. You have, or will pay for, ongoing developer support.

Shopify for B2B: faster to launch, tighter rules

Shopify takes the opposite approach. It's hosted, you don't patch servers, and a store can go live quickly. Its B2B features (companies with several locations, catalogues with their own prices, payment terms, quantity rules, volume pricing) were for a long time limited to Shopify Plus. Shopify has been changing which plans include which B2B features, so check the current plan pages before you budget.

What we like about Shopify for wholesale is how little there is to maintain. A small team can run it. Checkout is fast and reliable, and buyers who also shop on Shopify stores in their personal lives already know how it works.

What you give up is depth. You work inside Shopify's data model. If your rules fit it, great. If they don't, you're building an app, writing checkout logic with Shopify Functions, or accepting a compromise. Functions can handle a fair amount (custom discounts, payment and delivery rules, cart validation), but there are limits on what they can see and do, and you can't just rewrite the checkout however you like.

Shopify makes sense when:

  1. You want to launch in weeks, not months.
  2. Your B2B rules are fairly standard: a price list per company, terms, minimums.
  3. You'd rather pay a monthly fee than run hosting and upgrades.

If you're halfway there and need a few custom pieces, that's the kind of work on our Shopify development page: custom apps, checkout rules and theme changes.

The part people decide last but should decide first: the ERP

Here's an opinion: for B2B, the store is the easier half. The hard half is what happens after the order is placed.

Wholesale orders carry more information than retail ones. There's a company, a buyer, a delivery address that differs from the billing address, a payment term, a customer PO number, and a price that has to match what your ERP says it should be. If the store and the ERP disagree on any of that, someone in the office fixes it by hand. Usually that's the same person who used to retype orders, so you haven't saved much.

Before choosing a platform, we'd sit down and decide which system is the master for each kind of data:

  • Customers and companies. In Odoo, a business is a company contact with child contacts for each buyer and each delivery address. Your store's company accounts have to map to that structure cleanly, or you'll end up with duplicate contacts named "John Smith (2)".
  • Prices. Ideally these live in Odoo under Sales > Configuration > Pricelists and go out to the store. Keeping two sets of prices in sync by hand is where most of the errors we see come from.
  • Payment terms. Set on the customer in Odoo (the Sales & Purchase tab on the contact), then reflected on the store so buyers see the right terms at checkout.
  • Stock. Odoo is almost always the master. If you run several warehouses, decide which ones feed the store's stock figure.
  • Taxes. Fiscal positions in Odoo handle tax mapping per customer or country. The store's tax setup has to agree with them, otherwise the invoice amount won't match the order amount, and your accountant will notice.

What usually goes wrong? Rounding differences on tiered prices, tax calculated differently in the two systems, partial shipments that the store doesn't know about, and refunds processed in one place and never in the other. None of these are hard to fix, but you have to plan for them on day one rather than finding them in the first month-end close.

For Shopify, our Shopify Connector for Odoo syncs orders, stock, fulfilments, refunds, returns, payouts and abandoned checkouts between the store and Odoo. For Magento, we build Magento to Odoo integration as part of our Magento work, which tends to suit Magento stores better anyway, since most of them have been customised.

So which one?

If your B2B rules are standard and you want low upkeep, start with Shopify. If your catalogue or pricing is complicated and you're prepared to own the platform, Magento is the better tool. Either way, pick your ERP data model first. A clean integration matters more than which logo is on the storefront.

Questions we get asked

Can we run retail and wholesale on the same store?

Yes, on both platforms. Shopify handles it with B2B catalogues and company accounts alongside your retail storefront. Magento handles it with customer groups, shared catalogs or separate store views. The ERP side needs care, because retail and trade orders often go through different pricelists and payment terms in Odoo.

Is it hard to move from Magento to Shopify later, or the other way?

Products and customers move fine. Custom logic doesn't. Anything you built as a Magento module has to be rebuilt as a Shopify app or Function, and some of it may not be possible. If your ERP holds the master data, moving is much less painful, because the store becomes the thing you swap out.

Do we need an ERP at all for a small wholesale business?

Not on day one. But once you have more than one sales channel or more than one warehouse, keeping stock and invoices right in the store's admin gets painful quickly. We'd at least plan the data model so connecting Odoo later doesn't mean cleaning everything up first.
