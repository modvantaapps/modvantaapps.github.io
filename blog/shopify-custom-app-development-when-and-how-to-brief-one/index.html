Shopify Custom App Development: When and How to Brief One

When a store should pay for Shopify custom app development instead of an App Store subscription, and how to write a brief that gets a usable quote.

Picture a store that sells workshop equipment to trade customers. Retail prices for walk-up buyers, negotiated prices for a few hundred accounts, and a sales rep who still builds quotes in a spreadsheet because the site cannot do it.

Eleven apps installed. One for B2B pricing, one for quantity breaks, one to hide certain payment methods from wholesale accounts, one to tag orders so the warehouse knows what ships on a pallet. The subscriptions add up to real money every month, and the checkout still offers the wrong shipping option to half the trade accounts. Somebody on the team finally asks the question: would it be cheaper to have one app built for us?

Sometimes yes. Often no. Here is how we decide, and what we need from you before anyone can put a number on it.

Start by assuming you do not need one

We say this to people who are about to pay us, so take it seriously. The Shopify App Store is good. Reviews, subscriptions, inventory sync with the usual marketplaces, bundles, loyalty: all of that is solved. An app that thousands of stores use has been tested in ways your custom build never will be, and the vendor absorbs the cost of keeping it current when Shopify changes an API.

A custom app earns its keep when the requirement is specific to how your business makes money. If a competitor could install the same app and get the same result, buy the app.

The test we use: write down what you need in one paragraph, then search the App Store for it. If three apps do roughly 80% of it, buy the closest one and spend the rest of the budget on the missing 20%. If everything you try does 60% and the missing 40% is the part that matters, that is where Shopify custom app development starts to make sense. We build these on our Shopify page, usually alongside theme changes and checkout rules, because those three tend to arrive together.

The four situations where we build

Your pricing or checkout rules are yours alone. Price per customer group, minimum order value by region, free shipping only when the cart holds no oversized items, payment methods restricted by account type. Shopify Functions cover a lot of this now, running on Shopify's side at checkout instead of as a script the shopper can skip. This is the most common reason stores come to us.

Data has to land somewhere else and stay correct. Orders into an ERP, stock back from the warehouse, invoices out of accounting with the right numbering. Generic connectors move records. They do not know that your available stock has to subtract goods already allocated to a pending delivery route. We do plenty of this as Shopify to Odoo integration, and the apps and industries pages show the kind of back office systems we normally connect to.

You are stacking apps to fake one workflow. Four apps, three sets of tags, a Flow automation holding it together, and a person who checks every morning that it ran. That stack has a maintenance cost even though nobody puts it on a line item.

The real work happens in the admin, not the storefront. A packing screen for the warehouse, a bulk pricing tool for sales, a dashboard showing which route a day's orders belong to. Custom apps embed inside Shopify admin, so nobody has to learn a second login.

What you are actually buying

A custom Shopify app is not a plugin dropped into the theme. It is a small web application that you host, which Shopify loads inside the admin, talks to the GraphQL Admin API, listens to webhooks for events like order creation or product update, and in the checkout case ships Functions that Shopify runs on its own infrastructure.

What that means in practice:

  • It needs hosting, a database, logs, and someone watching it. Budget for that, not only the build.
  • Shopify releases a new API version every quarter and supports each one for roughly a year. Code left alone will break. Not maybe.
  • Webhooks arrive twice and arrive out of order. Anything that writes to another system has to be safe to run again, or you will double-count stock.
  • Rate limits are real. Bulk jobs should use Shopify's bulk operations instead of a loop that fires thousands of calls and gets throttled during your busiest hour.

How to brief one so the quote is useful

Most briefs we receive describe a feature. The ones that lead to an accurate fixed price describe a workflow. Try this order:

  1. Write the process as sentences, in the order it happens today. "The rep opens the order, checks the customer's agreed discount in the spreadsheet, applies it, then emails the PDF." Boring is fine. Boring is what we need.
  2. Name the fields. Which customer record holds the discount. Which metafield, or whether it currently lives in tags because nobody set up a metafield. Exact names, not "the customer data".
  3. Say who does it and how often. Two people, forty times a day, is a different build from one person, twice a week.
  4. Give volumes. SKU count, orders per day, peak month. This shapes the architecture more than the feature list does.
  5. Decide the edge cases before we ask. Partial refund on a bundled item. Customer in a region you do not ship to. Order edited after the warehouse printed the label. Someone in your business already knows the answers, so write them down.
  6. Say what must not change. An app you are keeping, the accounting export format, a report finance will not give up.

Two pages of that is enough to quote properly. Without it, every estimate you get is a guess wearing a number, and the gap turns up later as change requests.

What usually goes wrong

Variant limits per product have moved around over the years, so check the current figure before designing a product structure that depends on it. Checkout customisation has shifted to checkout extensibility, so older checkout.liquid tricks are gone and a few things that used to be simple now need a Function or a UI extension. Lower plans do not get all of it, which is a budget conversation rather than a technical one.

Tax is where custom builds get expensive quietly. Rules on digital goods, marketplace liability, and registration thresholds change regularly and differ by country. We will build whatever logic your accountant specifies, but we will not invent the rule for you. Confirm the treatment with a local accountant or your tax authority first, then tell us what to implement.

And the quiet one: nobody owns the app after launch. Agree on day one who receives the deprecation notices from Shopify. A small retainer or a planned yearly review costs less than an emergency.

Questions we get asked

Can one app replace five subscriptions?

Sometimes. Add up the subscriptions, then compare against build plus hosting plus a yearly update. If the payback lands inside a year or two and the logic is genuinely specific to your business, it usually adds up. If the only prize is the subscription saving, keep the apps.

Do we have to publish it on the App Store?

No. Most of what we build is a custom app installed on one store, which skips the public review process and keeps your logic private. Distributing publicly is a different project with its own rules.

Can it talk to our ERP?

Yes, and that is the half people underestimate. Field mapping and error handling take longer than the Shopify side does. If the back office system is not settled yet, our services page covers implementation and migration work as well.
