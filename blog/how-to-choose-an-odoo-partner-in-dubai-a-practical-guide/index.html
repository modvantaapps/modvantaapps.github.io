How to Choose an Odoo Partner in Dubai: A Practical Guide

A practical guide to picking an Odoo partner in Dubai, with vendor questions, VAT 201 and Corporate Tax readiness checks, and a scoping checklist.

You have decided on Odoo. Now you have six proposals, three of them from companies you have never heard of, and every one of them says the same things: certified consultants, fast delivery, full support. Two quote 180 hours, one quotes 600, and nobody has explained the difference. Meanwhile your accountant is asking who will make sure the VAT return still reconciles after the migration, and whether the new system will stand up to a Corporate Tax review. This is the real problem with searching for an Odoo partner in Dubai — the market is crowded, and the proposals are hard to compare.

This guide is about how to compare them. It is written for owners and finance or operations managers who will have to live with the result.

Separate the three things you are buying

Most Odoo projects fail on the boundary between these, so name them early.

  1. Configuration. Chart of accounts, taxes, warehouses, pricelists, approval rules, user rights. This is where most of the value is, and most of the risk.
  2. Custom development. Modules written for your process because standard Odoo does not cover it. Each one is a thing you own and maintain forever.
  3. Data and deployment. Moving history out of your old system, server setup, backups, upgrades.

A good team will tell you which of your requirements belong in each bucket, and will try to move items from bucket two into bucket one. A team that quotes custom development for everything is either not listening or is selling hours. Our own Odoo implementation work is structured this way — configuration, data import, user rights, custom modules and server deployment, with a demo database so you can test before anything goes live.

Write your own scope before you take demos

Vendors will happily shape your scope for you. Do this first instead. It takes a morning and it changes every conversation afterwards.

  • List your legal entities, branches and emirates of operation.
  • List the currencies you invoice and buy in.
  • Name the three reports your management actually reads each month.
  • Write down who approves a purchase order, a discount, a credit note.
  • Note every system that must keep working: bank, payment gateway, courier, e-commerce, payroll, POS.
  • Count your users by role, not by headcount.

Now you can ask every vendor to quote the same thing. You will be surprised how different the answers become.

The tax question most shortlists get wrong

In the UAE, two things sit on top of your Odoo setup: VAT and Corporate Tax. Both depend on bookkeeping quality rather than on clever reporting, and both are easier to get right at implementation than to retrofit a year later. Rules in this area change, and the detail depends on your licence, free zone status and activity — confirm the specifics with your local accountant or with the Federal Tax Authority before you commit to a design.

VAT 201 readiness

The return is produced from your posted invoices and bills. That means the tax codes on your products, customers and suppliers have to be right from day one, and your emirate data has to be captured on the documents rather than guessed at month end.

Ask a prospective partner how they intend to produce the figures. "We export to Excel and the accountant adjusts it" is a common answer and a poor one, because the adjustments are invisible by the second quarter. Our UAE VAT201 return app for Odoo 19 takes the opposite approach: it computes the FTA VAT201 boxes, emirate by emirate, from your own posted invoices and bills, and lets you open the journal items behind every figure. Whether you use that or something else, insist on that property — every number should be clickable back to a document.

Practical checks to raise in the scoping call:

  • Which tax codes will be set up, and who maintains them afterwards?
  • How are reverse charge and out-of-scope transactions handled?
  • How will designated zone or export transactions be flagged on documents?
  • What happens to an invoice that is corrected after the period is reported?

Corporate Tax readiness

Corporate Tax is less about a form and more about whether your books can support the position you take. The system work is unglamorous: a clean chart of accounts, proper separation between entities, related-party transactions recorded as such rather than netted off, fixed asset and depreciation handling that someone can explain, and an audit trail that survives staff turnover.

Ask the vendor whether the design supports entity-level reporting from the start, or whether that is a phase two. The honest answer is useful either way. Again, the tax treatment itself is your accountant's call — your job is to make sure the system does not stand in their way.

Questions to ask before you sign

Bring these to the second meeting, when the demo is over.

  1. Who exactly will do the work — names, roles, hours each?
  2. Which Odoo version, and what is your position on upgrades?
  3. Of my requirements, which are standard configuration and which need custom code?
  4. What does the data migration cover — open balances only, or history, and how many years?
  5. How will the VAT return be produced, and can I trace each figure to a document?
  6. What does testing look like, and do I get a demo database to try things in?
  7. What happens in the first month after go-live, and what does it cost?
  8. Who owns the custom modules and the source code?
  9. Where is the server, who has access, and how are backups tested?
  10. If we part ways, what do I walk away with?

Answers that are specific are worth more than answers that are enthusiastic.

Warning signs

  • A fixed price for a scope nobody has written down.
  • A proposal with no named people.
  • Heavy customisation proposed before anyone has looked at your current process.
  • No demo database, no test phase, no written acceptance step.
  • Reluctance to discuss code ownership or server access.
  • Support quoted as "included" with no hours, response time or channel.

A selection process that works

Keep it to three weeks. Send your written scope to four vendors. Take demos from the three who answer it properly rather than sending a brochure. Ask each to walk through one of your real processes — your actual quotation to cash flow, with your approval rules. Then ask for a phased proposal: a first phase that gets you live on finance, sales and inventory, and a second phase for the rest. A partner who pushes back on a sensible phasing plan is telling you something.

Check that the team understands your sector before you check anything else. Retail with instalment payments, trading with multi-currency landed costs and project businesses all break Odoo in different places. Our industries pages and the wider service list show how we group this work. If you sell online across the GCC, the detail matters at the checkout too — our Tabby payment gateway for Odoo 19 lets customers in Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Kuwait split a purchase into four interest-free payments inside your Odoo eCommerce checkout.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a partner based in Dubai?

Not necessarily. You need a team that understands UAE tax and trading practice, overlaps with your working hours, and answers quickly. Many implementations now run remotely, with on-site visits only where they add something. Judge the overlap and the responsiveness, not the postcode.

Can we start small and expand later?

Yes, and it is usually the better route. Go live on finance, sales and inventory first, run a full reporting cycle, then add manufacturing, projects or e-commerce. Phasing keeps the first budget honest and gives your team time to learn.

What should we prepare before the project starts?

Clean master data. Customers, suppliers, products, opening balances and tax codes. Nothing slows an implementation more than a product list nobody has tidied since 2019. Ask your partner for the import templates early and start filling them.
